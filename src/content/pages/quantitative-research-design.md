## The Four Main Quantitative Design Types

Before you can name a statistical test, you need to know what kind of design your study actually is:

- **Experimental**: participants are randomly assigned to conditions, and a variable is deliberately manipulated. This is the only design type that supports strong causal claims.
- **Quasi-experimental**: groups are compared, but without random assignment, common in education, organisational, and applied research where randomly assigning people isn't practical or ethical.
- **Correlational**: variables are measured as they naturally occur, with no manipulation, testing whether they relate to each other.
- **Descriptive**: the goal is to characterise a sample or population (means, frequencies, proportions), without testing a relationship or difference at all.

## How Design and Measurement Level Jointly Determine Your Test

Your design type narrows the field, but it's the combination of design and each variable's measurement level (nominal, ordinal, or continuous/scale) that actually points to a specific test. A quasi-experimental design comparing two groups on a continuous outcome calls for an independent samples t-test or ANCOVA; the same design comparing two groups on a categorical outcome calls for chi-square instead. A correlational design relating two continuous variables calls for Pearson correlation or regression; the same design with an ordinal variable calls for Spearman's rho.

This is why "what test do I use" is really two questions stacked together, not one: what's my design, and what's each variable's measurement level. Answer both, and the test is usually determined rather than chosen.
