## A Practical Self-Study Path: Interface, Descriptives, Then Inferential Tests

Trying to learn everything at once is the most common way students get overwhelmed by SPSS. A more workable order:

1. **Interface familiarisation first.** Learn Data View vs Variable View, how to enter and label data, and what the Measure setting actually controls, before running a single test.
2. **Descriptive statistics second.** Means, frequencies, and basic charts (Analyze > Descriptive Statistics) build comfort with the menu system and the Output Viewer without the added complexity of assumptions or hypothesis testing.
3. **Inferential tests third.** Once the interface and descriptives feel routine, move to t-tests, ANOVA, correlation, and regression, learning each one's specific assumptions as you go rather than trying to memorise all of them upfront.

## Getting Access: University Licence vs Trial Version

Most universities provide free student access to a full SPSS Statistics licence through their IT department; check there first before paying for anything. IBM also offers a free trial version with a time limit, useful for a short project if institutional access genuinely isn't available, though it's not a sustainable option for an entire degree's worth of work.

## Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

- **Leaving the Measure setting wrong.** A continuous variable left at Nominal after import will silently disappear from tests that require a continuous outcome.
- **Ignoring assumption checks.** Running a test and reporting the p-value without checking whether its assumptions actually held is the single most common source of lost marks.
- **Not saving syntax or documenting steps.** Without a record of exactly what you ran, reproducing or explaining your own analysis later becomes unnecessarily hard.
- **Treating output as self-explanatory.** SPSS output tables assume you already know which row and column to read; guessing instead of learning the correct one leads to real interpretation errors.
