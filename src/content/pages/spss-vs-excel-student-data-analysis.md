## What Excel Can and Can't Do for Inferential Statistics

Excel handles descriptive statistics competently: means, standard deviations, simple charts, and basic pivot tables. Where it falls short is inferential statistics, the tests that let you draw conclusions beyond your immediate sample. Excel has no built-in assumption diagnostics (no automatic Levene's test, no Shapiro-Wilk, no VIF for multicollinearity), which means checking a test's assumptions requires manual formulas that are easy to get subtly wrong. Running a t-test or ANOVA in Excel is possible with the Data Analysis ToolPak, but the output doesn't tell you whether your assumptions actually held, and building anything beyond the most basic test means hand-writing formulas with real risk of error.

## What SPSS Adds Beyond Descriptive Work

SPSS is built specifically for the inferential side: every major test comes with its assumption checks available in the same dialog box, output is structured and consistent across procedures, and post-hoc tests, effect sizes, and confidence intervals are a checkbox away rather than a formula you have to construct yourself. The tradeoff is that SPSS requires learning its own interface and terminology, and (outside a student licence) costs money where Excel is already on most people's computers.

## When It's Time to Move From Excel to SPSS

If your assignment asks for anything beyond means, frequencies, and a chart, an independent samples t-test, a correlation with a reported p-value, any kind of regression, Excel is the wrong tool even if it's technically possible to force it to produce a number. The risk isn't just extra effort; it's a manual formula error that looks plausible but is quietly wrong, with no assumption diagnostics to catch it.
