## Menu-Driven vs Code-Driven: The Core Difference

SPSS runs almost entirely through point-and-click menus, with syntax as an optional layer underneath for reproducibility. R is code-first: every analysis is written as a script, with no menu-driven equivalent for most procedures. For a student who's comfortable navigating dialog boxes and reading structured output tables, SPSS gets you to a result faster. For a student comfortable writing and debugging code, R offers more flexibility and, for advanced or unusual analyses, often more capability.

## Learning Curve and Time Investment

SPSS's learning curve is shorter for standard tests: if your dissertation needs a t-test, ANOVA, regression, or factor analysis, the menu path is discoverable without prior programming experience. R's learning curve is steeper up front, since you're learning a programming language alongside the statistics, but that investment pays off if your research involves highly customised analyses, simulations, or visualisations that go beyond what SPSS's menus offer.

## Cost and Licensing

R is free and open-source. SPSS requires a licence, though most universities provide student access at no direct cost through an institutional agreement. If you'll need statistical software beyond graduation and won't have institutional access, that's a real factor worth weighing; if your access is guaranteed through your programme, it matters less.

## When Your Committee or Field Expects One Over the Other

Field and committee convention often decides this before personal preference does. Psychology, education, nursing, and business programmes lean heavily toward SPSS, both in what committees expect to see and what's taught in required methods courses. Fields with stronger computational or open-science norms (parts of biostatistics, some economics and data-science-adjacent programmes) lean more toward R. Check what your specific programme and committee actually expect before deciding, since a technically sound analysis in the "wrong" tool for your field can still raise unnecessary questions at defence.
